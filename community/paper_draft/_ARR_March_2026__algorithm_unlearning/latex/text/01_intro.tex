% Privacy regulations and the right to erasure create concrete demand for machine unlearning: the ability to remove specific facts from a trained model without retraining from scratch. In LLMs, however, removing a fact is not symmetric with inserting it. Facts encountered frequently during pretraining are encoded redundantly across many layers and attention heads, making them far more resistant to gradient-based removal than rarely seen facts. Existing algorithms treat all facts in the forget set as equally hard to erase, producing a systematic popularity gap: methods calibrated for rare facts over-erase them while under-erasing popular ones, and methods calibrated for popular facts risk collateral damage to rare knowledge and general capabilities \cite{anna2026anatomy, krishnannot}.

% Gradient Ascent (GA, \cite{jang2022knowledge}) maximizes loss on the forget set without constraint, which frequently leads to catastrophic collapse of linguistic coherence. Gradient Difference (GD, \cite{liu2022continual}) adds a retain loss but still treats every forget token as equally important. Negative Preference Optimization (NPO, \cite{zhang2024negative}) reformulates unlearning as preference alignment, yielding a conservative signal that fails to erase well-memorised popular facts. Weighted Gradient Ascent (WGA, \cite{wang2025rethinking}) weights gradient updates by the model's own log-probabilities, focusing ascent on high-confidence tokens. All four methods rely on local model confidence as their primary signal, and none adjusts the forget-retain balance dynamically. The result is a suboptimal tradeoff: methods must be hand-tuned per dataset and model to avoid either insufficient forgetting or retain-set degradation. Our central claim is that log-probabilities alone do not accurately reflect how deeply a fact is embedded in the model~\cite{hartmann2024undesirable, kandpal2023large}; an external global popularity proxy provides a more reliable signal for calibrating unlearning strength.
